# Lesson 5 — Techniques

> **Goal:** understand the names of the core techniques, learn the pairs irimi/tenkan, omote/ura, soto/uchi and uke/tori, and finish hiragana with small letters and long vowels. After this lesson you can write any word in hiragana.

## 1. The numbered pins: *-kyō*

**Kyō** (教) means "teaching". The pins are simply numbered lessons:

| Technique | Parts | Literal |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **ikkyō** | ichi + kyō | first teaching |
| **nikyō** | ni + kyō | second teaching |
| **sankyō** | san + kyō | third teaching |
| **yonkyō** | yon + kyō | fourth teaching (not *shi*!) |
| **gokyō** | go + kyō | fifth teaching |
| **rokkyō** | roku + kyō | sixth teaching (in some styles) — also called **hiji-kime-osae**, "elbow-lock pin" (*hiji* = elbow) |

Notice: *ichi* + *kyō* → **ikkyō**, *roku* + *kyō* → **rokkyō**. The final vowel drops and the consonant doubles. This is a common sound change with numbers — you'll see it in *ippon* (one long thing), *hassen* (8,000), and more.

## 2. The throws: *-nage*

| Technique | Parts | Literal |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **shihō-nage** | shi (four) + hō (direction) + nage | four-direction throw |
| **irimi-nage** | iri (enter) + mi (body) + nage | entering-body throw |
| **kaiten-nage** | kaiten (rotation) + nage | rotation throw — done *soto* (outside) or *uchi* (inside), see section 3 |
| **tenchi-nage** | ten (heaven) + chi (earth) + nage | heaven-and-earth throw |
| **koshi-nage** | koshi (hips) + nage | hip throw |
| **kokyū-nage** | kokyū (breath) + nage | breath throw |
| **jūji-nage** | jūji (cross, lit. "the character 十") + nage | cross throw |
| **jūji-garami** | jūji (cross) + **g**arami (entangling) | cross entanglement: another name for *jūji-nage* |
| **ude-kime-nage** | ude (arm) + kime (lock) + nage | arm-lock throw |
| **sumi-otoshi** | sumi (corner) + otoshi (drop) | corner drop |
| **kote-gaeshi** | kote (wrist) + kaeshi (turn over) | wrist turn-out |

Fun fact: *jūji* is literally "the character for ten" — because 十 looks like a cross. Uke's arms end up crossed like 十 and locked against each other.

**Garami** comes from the verb 絡む *karamu*, "to entangle, to wrap around". The stem *karami* becomes a noun (lesson 4), and in a compound it voices to ***g**arami*: rendaku again, like *kote-**g**aeshi*. So 十字絡み *jūji-garami* = "cross entanglement", which describes the lock. *Jūji-nage* ("cross throw") names the same technique by its result.

And *tenchi-nage* is a whole worldview in two characters: one hand to heaven, one to earth.

**Ude-kime-nage** 腕極め投げ is built from three words: *ude* 腕 (arm) + *kime* 極め (lock) + *nage* 投げ (throw). *Kime* is the stem of 極める *kimeru*, "to apply a lock" (some dojos write 決め, "to settle"). It's the same *kime* as in *hiji-kime-osae*, the elbow-lock pin. Heard quickly, the name can sound like "udeki menage". Splitting a name into the words you know (*ude* · *kime* · *nage*) is the best way to catch where the word breaks really are.

### Free practice: jiyū-waza and randori

| Term | Kanji | Literal | What it is |
|------|-------|---------|------------|
| **jiyū-waza** | 自由技 | free technique | Uke attacks, often with a set attack, and nage answers with **any** technique |
| **randori** | 乱取り | chaotic taking | Free practice against several attackers (some styles use the word for other kinds of free practice) |
| **ninin-gake** | 二人掛け | two-person attack | Two attackers |
| **sannin-gake** | 三人掛け | three-person attack | Three attackers |
| **taninzu-gake** | 多人数掛け | many-people attack | Many attackers |

**自由** *jiyū* is the everyday word for **freedom**: 自 (self) + 由 (reason, cause), "following your own reason". On a Japanese train, a 自由席 *jiyū-seki* is an unreserved, sit-where-you-like seat.

Two sound changes you already know:
- *ran* + *tori* → ran-**d**ori, and *kakeru* (to attack, to hang on) → -**g**ake: rendaku, as in lesson 4.
- *Waza* doesn't change, because rendaku never touches *w*.

### Three positions: tachi-waza, suwari-waza, hanmi handachi

| Term | Kanji | Literal | Who is standing? |
|------|-------|---------|------------------|
| **tachi-waza** | 立ち技 | standing techniques | Both |
| **suwari-waza** | 座り技 | sitting techniques | Neither: both kneel |
| **hanmi handachi** | 半身半立ち | half body, half standing | Uke stands, nage kneels |

*Tachi* is the stem of 立つ *tatsu*, "to stand" (lesson 7's *tatte kudasai*). *Suwari* is the stem of 座る *suwaru*, "to sit". In *han-dachi*, *tachi* voices to *dachi*: rendaku again.

Don't confuse 立ち *tachi* (standing) with 太刀 *tachi* (sword) from lesson 8. They sound the same but are written differently.

**Careful in hiragana:** 自由 is **じゆう** *ji-yu-u*, with a full-size ゆ (3 beats). Ten is **じゅう** *jū*, with a small ゅ (2 beats). One letter's size is the difference between "free technique" and "ten technique".

## 3. Pairs that shape every technique

Most techniques are named by an attack, a technique and a **variation**: *katate-dori ikkyō **omote***. Four pairs come up again and again.

### Irimi 入身 / Tenkan 転換 — entering and turning

| | Kanji | Literal | On the mat |
|-|-------|---------|------------|
| **irimi** | 入 enter + 身 body | "entering body" | Step *into* the attack, past uke's line, instead of backing away |
| **tenkan** | 転 turn + 換 change | "turn and change" | Pivot on the front foot, turning to face the same way uke faces |

Many dojos practise them together as the footwork drill **irimi-tenkan**. And you know 転 already: it's the *ten* in **回転投げ** *kaiten-nage*, "rotation throw".

Irimi and tenkan are the two basic kinds of **tai-sabaki** 体捌き, "body handling": *tai* 体 (body) + *sabaki* 捌き (handling, from 捌く *sabaku*, "to handle, to deal with"). Tai-sabaki is any way of moving your whole body to get off the line of attack and into a good position. Another drill you'll hear is **tai no henkō** 体の変更, "change of body": a turn on the spot, and the の is the one from lesson 3.

### Omote 表 / Ura 裏 — front and back

| | Kanji | Everyday meaning | On the mat |
|-|-------|------------------|------------|
| **omote** | 表 | the surface, the front side of a sheet of paper | Nage enters **in front of** uke |
| **ura** | 裏 | the reverse, the back, the lining | Nage moves **behind** uke, usually with a turn (*tenkan*) |

The words are everyday Japanese: the *ura* of a coin is its back, and 裏口 *uraguchi* ("back mouth") is a back door. Some styles use other names for the same idea: Yoshinkan, for example, numbers them **ichi** (1) and **ni** (2).

### Soto 外 / Uchi 内 — outside and inside

| | Kanji | Everyday meaning | On the mat |
|-|-------|------------------|------------|
| **soto** | 外 | outside, outdoors | Nage stays **outside** uke's arm |
| **uchi** | 内 | inside, within | Nage passes **inside**, often stepping under uke's arm |

You hear the pair in **soto-kaiten-nage** 外回転投げ (outside rotation throw) and **uchi-kaiten-nage** 内回転投げ (inside rotation throw). Also in the dojo: an **uchi-deshi** 内弟子 is a live-in student, literally an "inside disciple".

*Uchi-kaiten* is also an **entry** into other techniques, not only a throw. In **uchi-kaiten-sankyō** 内回転三教, nage turns in under uke's arm (*uchi-kaiten*) and comes out applying *sankyō*. The name is the sequence again, like *kata-dori men-uchi* in lesson 4: first the movement, then the technique.

Watch out: this **uchi** 内 (inside) sounds exactly like the **uchi** 打ち (strike) in *shōmen-uchi*. They're different words, and only the kanji tells them apart, just like 心 and 身 (both *shin*) in lesson 8. In speech, context does the job: *uchi-kaiten-nage* is a throw, so the *uchi* there can't be a strike.

### Uke 受け / Tori 取り — who receives, who takes

| | Kanji | From the verb | Role |
|-|-------|---------------|------|
| **uke** | 受け | 受ける *ukeru*, to receive | Attacks, then *receives* the technique and falls |
| **tori** | 取り | 取る *toru*, to take | Does the technique |

Uke is the attacker, but the name says *receiver*: uke receives the technique. The partner doing the technique has different names in different dojos:

| Name | Kanji | Literal |
|------|-------|---------|
| **tori** | 取り | the one who takes |
| **nage** | 投げ | the one who throws |
| **shite** | 仕手 | the one who does |

All three mean the same role, so use whichever your dojo uses. (*Shite* is built from *shi*, the stem of *suru* "to do", plus *te* "person", so it really is "the doer". It sounds exactly like して, the *-te* form of *suru*, which you'll meet in lesson 7.)

## 4. Reading corner: small letters

### Small ゃ ゅ ょ — combined sounds

A small **ゃ (ya)**, **ゅ (yu)** or **ょ (yo)** after an *i*-letter merges into one beat:

| | ゃ ya | ゅ yu | ょ yo |
|-|------|------|------|
| き ki | きゃ kya | きゅ kyu | きょ **kyo** |
| し shi | しゃ sha | しゅ shu | しょ **sho** |
| ち chi | ちゃ cha | ちゅ chu | ちょ cho |
| に ni | にゃ nya | にゅ nyu | にょ nyo |
| ひ hi | ひゃ hya | ひゅ hyu | ひょ hyo |
| み mi | みゃ mya | みゅ myu | みょ myo |
| り ri | りゃ rya | りゅ ryu | りょ **ryo** |
| ぎ gi | ぎゃ gya | ぎゅ gyu | ぎょ gyo |
| じ ji | じゃ ja | じゅ **ju** | じょ **jo** |
| び bi | びゃ bya | びゅ byu | びょ byo |
| ぴ pi | ぴゃ pya | ぴゅ pyu | ぴょ pyo |

Size matters: **きよ** = *ki-yo* (2 beats), **きょ** = *kyo* (1 beat).

### Small っ — the held breath

A small **っ** doubles the next consonant — a short pause, like the moment before *irimi*:

- **いっきょう** = *i-k-kyō*
- **ぼっけん** = *bo-k-ken* (wooden sword)
- **かって** = *ka-t-te*

### Long vowels in writing

Long vowels are written by adding a vowel:

| Long sound | Usually written | Example |
|------------|-----------------|---------|
| **ā** | ああ | おかあさん *okāsan* (mother) |
| **ī** | いい | いいえ *iie* (no) |
| **ū** | うう | こきゅう *kokyū* |
| **ē** | **えい** (most common) | せんせい *sensē* |
| **ō** | **おう** (most common) | どうじょう *dōjō* |

So the macrons in *dōjō* are not decoration — they're real letters: **ど・う・じょ・う**.

### Counting beats in hiragana

Now beat-counting is easy: **each full-size letter is one beat**.
- Small **ゃ ゅ ょ** don't count: they merge with the letter before them.
- Small **っ** *does* count: it's the pause.

- どうじょう: ど・う・じょ・う = **4 beats**
- いっきょう: い・っ・きょ・う = **4 beats**
- てんかん: て・ん・か・ん = **4 beats**

## Exercises

1. Why is it *ikkyō* and not *ichikyō*?
2. Literal meanings: *shihō-nage*, *irimi-nage*, *tenchi-nage*.
3. Count the beats: **しょうめんうち**, **いっきょう**, **りょうてどり**.
4. Read: **きょう**, **しゃしん**, **ちょっと**, **りょこう**, **がっこう**.
5. Write in hiragana: *kokyū*, *nikyō*, *dōjō*, *gokyō*, *ryōte-dori*.
6. In *ikkyō ura*, where does nage go: in front of uke or behind? Which footwork usually goes with it?
7. Uke attacks first, so why is uke called "the receiver"?
8. What are three names for the partner who does the technique?
9. In *uchi-kaiten-nage*, is *uchi* 内 or 打ち? How do you know?
10. Read and count the beats: **じゆうわざ** and **じゅうじなげ**. Which one has a small ゅ?

## On the mat

Before each technique, picture its name written in hiragana. *Shihō-nage* → しほうなげ. Writing it on the mat in your head is surprisingly effective.
